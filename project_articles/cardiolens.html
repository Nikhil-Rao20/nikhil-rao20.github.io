Abstract

The manual analysis of echocardiograms, a cornerstone of cardiac diagnostics, is often a time-consuming process prone to inter-observer variability and error. This project, CardioLens, introduces a robust deep learning framework for the automated segmentation of the left ventricle and subsequent calculation of the Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) from echocardiogram videos. Our dual-task pipeline utilizes a high-performing DeepLabV3 model with a ResNet-101 backbone for semantic segmentation and an R(2+1)D video model for LVEF estimation. A key contribution of this work is the systematic optimization of the entire workflow using the Intel® AI software stack. By leveraging the Intel® Extension for PyTorch (IPEX) for accelerated training and the Intel® Distribution of OpenVINO™ Toolkit for inference, we demonstrate a significant performance increase, including a nearly 50% reduction in inference time on standard Intel® CPUs compared to native PyTorch. The final segmentation model achieves a Dice Score of 0.92, providing a highly accurate, efficient, and accessible solution to augment clinical decision-making in cardiology.

Introduction

Cardiovascular Diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, making accurate and timely diagnosis a critical global health priority. Echocardiography stands as the most widely used non-invasive imaging modality for assessing cardiac function due to its safety and accessibility. A key biomarker derived from these scans is the Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF)—the percentage of blood pumped out of the left ventricle with each contraction. LVEF is a fundamental indicator of cardiac health and is crucial for diagnosing and managing conditions like heart failure.

Despite its importance, the clinical workflow for LVEF assessment faces significant challenges. The gold standard involves manual or semi-automated tracing of the left ventricular border by a sonographer. This process is inherently time-consuming, requiring significant effort that detracts from time that could be spent on direct patient care. Furthermore, the accuracy of the tracing is highly dependent on the operator's skill and experience, leading to high inter-observer and intra-observer variability. Studies indicate that diagnostic errors in echocardiography are common, with a significant percentage being preventable, often stemming from poor image quality or cognitive biases in interpretation. These inefficiencies create a bottleneck in cardiac care, potentially delaying diagnosis and increasing healthcare costs.

This project introduces CardioLens, an end-to-end automated framework designed to address these limitations. Our primary contribution is a dual-task AI pipeline that performs both accurate semantic segmentation of the left ventricle and calculates the LVEF from raw echocardiogram videos. We systematically trained and evaluated multiple state-of-the-art deep learning architectures to identify the optimal model for this clinical task. Finally, we demonstrate the critical role of the Intel® AI software stack (IPEX and OpenVINO™) in accelerating both training and inference, making the solution practical for deployment on widely available CPU hardware without compromising accuracy.

Methodology

Our methodology is centered around a comprehensive pipeline that automates the entire process from video input to diagnostic output. The study was conducted using the EchoNet-Dynamic Dataset, a large, publicly available collection of echocardiogram videos with associated labels for LVEF and tracings of the left ventricle at end-diastole and end-systole. The dataset was systematically split into training, validation, and testing sets to ensure robust model development and unbiased evaluation. Video files in .avi format were preprocessed by extracting individual frames, which were then resized and normalized to be suitable for model input.

The CardioLens architecture, shown in Figure 1, is designed to process an input echocardiogram video and output a full diagnostic analysis, including a segmentation mask video, an electrocardiogram (ECG) visualization, and the calculated LVEF.

Models and Training Details

To achieve precise segmentation of the left ventricle, we evaluated three distinct deep learning architectures: the transformer-based Intel DPT-Large model, and two variants of the popular CNN-based DeepLabV3 model, one with a lightweight MobileNetV3-Large backbone and another with a more powerful ResNet-101 backbone. These models were fine-tuned on the EchoNet-Dynamic dataset, and the DeepLabV3 with ResNet-101 architecture was ultimately selected for its superior performance.

Following segmentation, the Ejection Fraction is calculated using the area-length method on the end-diastolic and end-systolic frames. To handle the temporal nature of the video data for this task, we employed an 18-layer R(2+1)D model, a 3D CNN architecture adept at learning spatio-temporal features. This model processes sequences of frames to accurately estimate the End-Diastolic Volume (EDV) and End-Systolic Volume (ESV), which are then used to calculate the LVEF via the formula: EF = (EDV - ESV) / EDV. The model also generates a corresponding ECG video to visualize the heart's rhythm.

Intel Technologies

A core objective was to ensure the framework is not only accurate but also computationally efficient. This was achieved through an end-to-end optimization strategy using Intel® technologies. For training, the Intel® Extension for PyTorch (IPEX) was used to optimize PyTorch code with oneAPI and oneDNN primitives, enabling more efficient use of Intel hardware to significantly reduce training time. For deployment, the trained PyTorch models were converted to the OpenVINO™ Intermediate Representation (IR) format. The Intel® Distribution of OpenVINO™ Toolkit optimizes deep learning models for inference on Intel hardware, applying techniques like graph pruning, quantization, and kernel fusion. This resulted in a dramatic reduction in inference latency, making real-time analysis on standard CPUs feasible.

Experiments & Results

Performance Metrics

All experiments were conducted on a system equipped with a 12th-Gen Intel® Core™ i7-12650H CPU. The framework was built using Python with core libraries including PyTorch, TorchVision, Hugging Face Transformers, OpenCV, and the Intel® AI Analytics Toolkit. Model performance was evaluated on segmentation accuracy using the Dice Coefficient, model loss using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE), and computational performance measured by Inference Time in seconds.

The rigorous evaluation of segmentation models, detailed in Table 1, established the superiority of the DeepLabV3 with ResNet-101 architecture, which achieved an outstanding overall Dice Score of 0.9209. This high score indicates a very high degree of overlap with the ground truth annotations provided by expert cardiologists.

Discussion

Our experiments confirm that deep learning models, particularly CNN architectures like DeepLabV3 with a ResNet backbone, can achieve expert-level accuracy in segmenting the left ventricle. More importantly, the results highlight that this level of accuracy can be paired with high performance. The ~50% reduction in inference time achieved with OpenVINO™ is a critical finding, as it demonstrates that complex AI analyses can be run efficiently on ubiquitous CPU hardware, lowering the barrier to entry for clinical adoption. The CardioLens framework has the potential to be a transformative tool in clinical cardiology. By providing an automated, accurate, and fast "second opinion," it can help reduce diagnostic errors, improve consistency across different operators, and free up valuable clinician time. Its low computational footprint makes it suitable for integration into existing Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) in hospitals or as a standalone educational tool for medical students and sonography trainees.

While the results are promising, the current model has been trained and validated on adult echocardiograms. A significant area for future work is to extend and validate this framework for the analysis of pediatric and infant cardiac data, which presents unique challenges due to different heart rates and sizes. We also plan to expand the model's capabilities to detect other cardiac abnormalities beyond LVEF, further increasing its clinical utility.

Conclusion

The CardioLens project successfully developed and validated an AI-powered framework for the automated analysis of echocardiograms. We demonstrated that by selecting an appropriate deep learning architecture and leveraging hardware-aware optimization toolkits like the Intel® Distribution of OpenVINO™, it is possible to build a system that is both highly accurate and computationally efficient. This work represents a significant step towards the practical integration of AI into routine clinical workflows, with the potential to enhance diagnostic quality, improve patient outcomes, and make advanced cardiac care more accessible.
