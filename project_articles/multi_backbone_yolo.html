1. Abstract

The You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models, culminating in YOLOv8, represents the state-of-the-art in real-time object detection, primarily due to its meticulously balanced architecture. The backbone, responsible for feature extraction, is a critical determinant of this balance between speed and accuracy. This paper presents a comprehensive architectural exploration into enhancing the YOLOv8 framework by replacing its standard CSPDarkNet backbone with a diverse set of modern architectures, including the classic ResNet50, Vision Transformers (DeiT, NextViT), and emerging State Space Models (MambaVision, VMamba, Vision Mamba). Our methodology focuses on the seamless integration of these backbones to produce multi-scale feature maps compatible with the YOLOv8 neck. While full-scale training was precluded by computational resource limitations, we provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of the resulting models. We present a detailed comparison of model complexity through parameter counts and Giga Floating-Point Operations Per Second (GFLOPs) across various model sizes, from Nano to Extra Large. This analysis serves as a valuable blueprint for future research, offering insights into the potential trade-offs and benefits of leveraging next-generation vision architectures to push the boundaries of the YOLO framework.

2. Introduction

Since its introduction by Redmon et al. [1], the You Only Look Once (YOLO) model has revolutionized real-time object detection. By framing detection as a single regression problem, YOLO broke away from the slow, multi-stage pipelines that previously dominated the field, enabling high-speed performance without a catastrophic loss in accuracy. This philosophy has been refined through numerous iterations, with YOLOv8 [2] standing as the current state-of-the-art, offering exceptional efficiency and flexibility for a range of vision tasks.

At the heart of any object detector lies its backbone—a deep neural network tasked with transforming raw input pixels into a rich hierarchy of semantic features. The performance of the detector is fundamentally tied to the representational power of its backbone. The default backbone in YOLOv8 is CSPDarkNet, an evolution of the DarkNet architecture that cleverly incorporates Cross Stage Partial (CSP) connections. This design enhances the learning process by improving gradient flow and reducing computational redundancy, allowing it to strike a formidable balance between speed and accuracy.

However, the field of computer vision is in constant flux. While CSPDarkNet is a highly optimized CNN, recent years have seen the rise of alternative architectural paradigms, most notably Vision Transformers (ViTs) [7] and, more recently, State Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba [6]. These architectures excel at modeling long-range dependencies and capturing global context, capabilities that are potentially complementary to the local feature extraction strengths of CNNs. This raises a critical research question: can the performance of a highly optimized detector like YOLOv8 be further enhanced by integrating these next-generation backbones?

This paper investigates this question through a systematic architectural study. We propose and design several novel variants of YOLOv8 where the CSPDarkNet backbone is replaced. Our contribution is a detailed blueprint and a theoretical comparative analysis of these new architectures, focusing on their complexity and computational cost. This work provides a foundational guide for practitioners and researchers looking to select or design advanced backbones tailored to specific performance requirements, paving the way for the next evolution of the YOLO framework.

3. Methodology

The core of our methodology is the surgical replacement of the YOLOv8 backbone while preserving the integrity of its subsequent components—the neck and the head. The YOLOv8 architecture relies on its backbone to produce feature maps at three distinct scales, representing low-level, mid-level, and high-level semantic information. These multi-scale features are then passed to the neck (typically a Path Aggregation Network or PANet), which fuses them to create a rich feature pyramid for detection at various sizes and resolutions.

Our primary technical task was to adapt each candidate backbone to generate these three specific feature maps. This involved identifying appropriate intermediate layers within each architecture that correspond to the desired spatial resolutions and feature granularities. Once extracted, these feature maps were seamlessly channeled into the YOLOv8 neck, creating a complete, functional, and novel detector architecture. This modular approach was also extended to develop a multitask version of the framework, capable of simultaneously handling object detection, instance segmentation, and pose estimation by leveraging the shared feature representations from the new backbone.

Backbone Architectures

The candidate backbones selected for this study represent a cross-section of modern computer vision architectures:

ResNet50

A foundational deep residual network [3], serving as a robust and well-understood CNN baseline. ResNet50 introduces skip connections that enable the training of very deep networks by addressing the vanishing gradient problem.

DeiT (Data-efficient Image Transformer)

A Vision Transformer variant designed for effective training without massive datasets [4]. DeiT demonstrates that transformers can achieve competitive performance with CNNs while requiring less training data through improved training strategies.

NextViT

A next-generation Vision Transformer that incorporates convolutional principles to achieve higher efficiency and performance [5]. NextViT bridges the gap between CNNs and Transformers by combining the benefits of both architectural paradigms.

Mamba-based Models (MambaVision, VMamba, Vision Mamba)

A family of recent architectures built upon State Space Models [6, 8, 9]. These models offer an alternative to the quadratic complexity of attention in Transformers, enabling linear-time sequence modeling and presenting a new frontier for vision backbones. They excel at capturing long-range dependencies with improved computational efficiency.

4. Experimental Setup and Theoretical Analysis

This section details the architectural configurations of our proposed models and presents a comparative analysis of their theoretical complexity. Due to significant computational resource constraints, full-scale training and evaluation on standard benchmarks like PASCAL VOC or COCO were not performed. Consequently, performance metrics such as mean Average Precision (mAP) are not included. The analysis is therefore focused on the architectural design and its implications for model size and computational cost.

For each of the seven backbone types (Vanilla CSPDarkNet, ResNet50, DeiT, NextViT, MambaVision, VisionMamba, and VMamba), we designed five model scales: Nano, Small, Medium, Large, and Extra Large. The complexity of these 35 variants was evaluated using two key metrics: the total number of parameters and the Giga Floating-Point Operations Per Second (GFLOPs).

5. Discussion

The results of our theoretical analysis provide a clear, albeit preliminary, roadmap for the future of YOLOv8 backbone design. The data presented in Table 1 and Table 2 highlights the fundamental trade-off between the complexity of a model and its potential representational capacity. The significantly higher parameter counts and GFLOPs of architectures like NextViT and VMamba suggest that they are engineered to learn more intricate and abstract features from data. We hypothesize that this increased capacity would translate into substantial accuracy gains on challenging datasets with small objects, heavy occlusion, or fine-grained categories, where the superior contextual modeling of Transformers and SSMs would be most impactful.

Key Trade-offs Identified

Computational Efficiency vs. Representational Power: While VMamba and NextViT models show the highest computational costs, they potentially offer superior feature learning capabilities for complex detection scenarios.

Memory Footprint vs. Accuracy: The parameter count analysis reveals that advanced architectures like Vision Transformers require significantly more memory, which may limit deployment in resource-constrained environments.

Architecture-Specific Scaling: Different backbone architectures exhibit unique scaling behaviors, with some showing linear increases in complexity while others demonstrate more exponential growth patterns.

Conversely, the continued efficiency of the vanilla CSPDarkNet and the relatively lightweight nature of the Vision Mamba models underscore their suitability for applications where resource constraints are paramount, such as real-time detection on edge devices. This analysis allows a practitioner to make an informed, a priori decision. For an application demanding maximum accuracy where latency is less critical (e.g., medical image analysis), a VMamba-based backbone might be the optimal choice. For a high-framerate mobile application, a VisionMamba or vanilla backbone would be more appropriate. Our work thus transforms the abstract concept of a "speed-accuracy trade-off" into a concrete set of architectural choices with quantifiable costs.

6. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper has presented a comprehensive design and theoretical analysis of integrating multiple next-generation backbones into the YOLOv8 framework. By systematically replacing the standard CSPDarkNet backbone and calculating the resulting model complexity, we have created a valuable blueprint for future research in object detection. Our analysis quantifies the trade-offs associated with leveraging advanced architectures like Vision Transformers and State Space Models, providing a guide for tailoring YOLOv8 to specific computational budgets and performance goals.

The clear and immediate next step is to secure the necessary computational resources to empirically validate these architectural designs. Our future work will involve training all 35 model variants on the COCO and PASCAL VOC datasets. This will allow for a full empirical evaluation, measuring critical performance metrics like mAP, Frames Per Second (FPS), and inference latency. This validation will confirm our hypotheses and provide the definitive data needed to crown the next generation of YOLO backbones. Furthermore, we plan to continue the development of the fully integrated multitask model, optimizing its training strategies to create a single, efficient, and versatile tool for comprehensive visual understanding.

Future Research Directions

  • Empirical Validation: Full-scale training and evaluation on standard datasets (COCO, PASCAL VOC)
  • Hardware-specific Optimization: Tailoring architectures for specific deployment environments (edge devices, cloud, mobile)
  • Hybrid Architectures: Exploring combinations of different backbone paradigms within a single model
  • Multitask Integration: Developing unified models for detection, segmentation, and pose estimation
  • Real-world Deployment: Practical implementation and testing in production environments
