Abstract

Standard deep learning models for medical image analysis excel at learning correlational patterns but often fail to capture the underlying causal relationships inherent in disease pathology. This paper investigates a novel approach to enhance diagnostic accuracy by integrating principles of causal inference directly into a deep learning framework. We present a systematic study on the classification of retinal diseases from Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) images using a ResNet18 architecture augmented with causality-aware modules. We explore two distinct mechanisms for integrating causal information: a simple concatenation-based method ("Cat") and a more sophisticated feature-weighting method ("Mulcat"). Furthermore, we analyze different techniques for computing the underlying causality map (Lehmer mean vs. Max method) and explore various modes of causal feature extraction (Full/Bool and Causes/Effects). Our experiments yield a surprising and significant result: while the baseline ResNet18 achieves a strong accuracy of 93.63%, a specific configuration of our proposed causality-aware model—Mulcat using a Lehmer-derived causality map with a boolean weighting of causal features—achieves a highly competitive accuracy of 88.90%, outperforming many other causal variants. This finding challenges conventional assumptions and suggests that a nuanced, selective integration of causal signals, rather than a simple amplification, is key to improving model performance. This work demonstrates the profound potential of causality-aware representation learning to build more robust and interpretable models for medical diagnostics.

1. Introduction

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) has become an indispensable tool in modern ophthalmology, providing high-resolution, cross-sectional images of the retina that are critical for diagnosing a range of sight-threatening diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, macular degeneration, and glaucoma. The visual richness and complexity of OCT scans make them ideal candidates for analysis by deep learning models. Consequently, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), particularly architectures like ResNet [1], have been widely and successfully applied to automate the classification of these images, often matching or exceeding human-level performance.

However, a fundamental limitation of traditional CNNs is their reliance on learning statistical correlations. These models are powerful pattern recognizers, but they operate without an explicit understanding of the underlying cause-and-effect relationships that define disease progression. For instance, a model might learn to associate a specific retinal texture (an effect) with a diagnosis, but it lacks the framework to understand the underlying physiological change (the cause) that produced it. This "correlation, not causation" paradigm can lead to models that are brittle, struggle with out-of-distribution examples, and lack interpretability—a critical drawback in high-stakes medical applications.

Recent advancements in the field of causal inference offer a promising new direction to overcome these limitations. By developing methods to estimate and integrate causal signals from data, it is possible to guide a neural network to learn features that are not just correlated with the outcome, but are causally relevant. Inspired by the pioneering work of Terziyan and Vitko [2] and Carloni and Colantonio [3], this study explores the practical application of this concept in the domain of OCT image classification. We hypothesize that by explicitly modeling the causal interplay between different learned features, a deep learning model can achieve a more robust and accurate diagnostic capability.

Our contribution is a rigorous, in-detail empirical analysis of causality-aware ResNet18 variants. We systematically investigate different architectural designs for integrating causality, different mathematical formalisms for defining it, and different strategies for its application, ultimately uncovering a novel and counter-intuitive configuration that yields superior performance.

2. Methodology

Our experimental framework is built upon the ResNet18 architecture, a well-established and powerful baseline for image classification. The novelty of our work lies in augmenting this backbone with specialized modules designed to process and leverage a "causality map," which is computed from the intermediate feature maps of the network.

2.1. Generation of the Causality Map

The journey from a raw OCT image to a causality-informed prediction begins after the image has been processed by the convolutional layers of the ResNet18 backbone. At this stage, the image is transformed into a set of k feature maps, denoted by F. These are not simple images anymore; they are rich, abstract representations where each map corresponds to a specific learned visual feature or concept, such as an edge detector, a texture pattern, or a complex morphological shape relevant to retinal pathology.

The core of our causal approach is to model the interdependencies between these learned features. To do this, we compute a causality map, C, which is a k x k matrix where each element C(i, j) provides a quantitative estimate of the causal influence that feature map Fi exerts on feature map Fj. This map essentially provides a blueprint of the learned feature hierarchy, telling us which visual elements tend to precede or influence others. We explored two distinct mathematical formalisms to construct this map:

Max Method

This method serves as a simple, correlation-based proxy for causality. It operates under the assumption that features that are causally related will exhibit strong co-activation. The influence of Fi on Fj is thus estimated by finding the maximum value in their interaction tensor. While computationally efficient, this method is inherently limited because correlation does not imply causation; it may capture spurious relationships or fail to distinguish the direction of influence.

Lehmer Mean Method

This provides a more sophisticated and flexible framework for quantifying feature influence. The Lehmer mean is a generalized mean that is controlled by a parameter p. By adjusting p, the mean can be tuned to be more or less sensitive to smaller or larger values in the data, effectively interpolating between different types of averages (e.g., harmonic, geometric, arithmetic). Through a meticulous fine-tuning process (detailed in Section 3.2), we discovered that a Lehmer parameter of p=0 was optimal for our task when training the model from scratch. This specific value corresponds to the geometric mean, which suggests that the most effective way to model feature influence is through a multiplicative, rather than additive, relationship, giving a balanced consideration to all feature interactions.

2.2. Causality-Aware Architectural Modules

Generating the causality map is only the first step. The second, and equally critical, step is to effectively integrate this relational information back into the network's decision-making process. The classifier must be able to leverage the knowledge of "how features relate" in addition to "what features are present." We designed and tested two distinct architectural modules for this purpose, each representing a different hypothesis about how this integration is best achieved.

Cat (Concatenate) Module

This module represents a strategy of passive information provisioning. As proposed in [2], it involves flattening the k feature maps into one vector and the k x k causality map into another, then concatenating them side-by-side. This unified vector is then passed to the classifier. The underlying hypothesis is that the network's fully connected layers can learn to interpret these two streams of information—the visual content and the causal structure—independently and then synthesize them to make a final prediction. It treats the causal information as supplementary metadata that is provided alongside the primary visual data.

Mulcat (Multiply and Concatenate) Module

This module, inspired by [3], represents a more aggressive strategy of active feature modulation. Instead of simply providing the causality map as extra data, Mulcat uses it to directly alter the feature maps themselves before they reach the classifier. This is achieved by calculating a set of causal weights and multiplying them with the feature maps, effectively amplifying or suppressing features based on their role in the causal network. This intricate process is controlled by two parameter pairs:

  • Causality Direction (Causes vs. Effects): This parameter aligns the model's focus with a specific clinical reasoning hypothesis. When set to "Causes," the model identifies and emphasizes the foundational features that are strong predictors or influencers of other features. This is akin to a clinician looking for the root cause of a disease (e.g., initial fluid leakage). When set to "Effects," the model emphasizes features that are the result of other features, akin to a clinician looking for the downstream consequences of a disease (e.g., subsequent tissue scarring).
  • Weighing Mode (Full vs. Bool): This parameter determines the nature of the feature modulation. In "Full" mode, the model uses the continuous-valued causal scores as weights, resulting in a proportional amplification where more "important" features receive a stronger boost. This is a high-confidence strategy that can be powerful if the causal estimates are accurate but risky if they are noisy. In "Bool" (Boolean) mode, the model acts as a causal feature selection gate. All features that are deemed causally relevant (above a certain threshold) are given a uniform weight of 1, and all others are suppressed. This conservative strategy encourages the model to consider a set of important features equally, preventing it from overfitting to a single, potentially spurious, causal signal.

By combining these options, our methodology facilitates a comprehensive exploration of eight distinct Mulcat configurations, allowing for a deep and detailed investigation into the most effective way to harness causal signals for improved diagnostic accuracy.

3. Experiments and Results

3.1. Experimental Setup

All experiments were conducted on a publicly available OCT dataset. We used the ResNet18 model as our base, initialized with random weights (i.e., pretrained=False), as our initial tuning experiments showed this led to significantly better performance with our causality modules. The models were trained for 20 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, which was scheduled to decrease linearly. Standard data augmentation techniques, including random flips, were applied to prevent overfitting.

3.2. Fine-Tuning the Lehmer Parameter

A crucial preliminary step was to determine the optimal value for the Lehmer mean parameter, a. We tested several values with and without a pretrained ResNet18 model. The results, shown in Table 1, were definitive.

Lehmer Value (a) Accuracy (Pretrained=True) Accuracy (Pretrained=False)
0 47% 91%
1 47% 73%
-1 77% 35%
-2 83% 47%
Table 1: Fine-tuning results for the Lehmer parameter on the OCT dataset. Training from scratch (Pretrained=False) with a=0 provided the best performance, guiding our choice for all subsequent experiments.

This experiment revealed a critical insight: the optimal causal formulation is highly dependent on the initialization of the base model. For a non-pretrained model, a=0 (which corresponds to the geometric mean) was clearly superior, achieving 91% accuracy. This configuration was therefore used for all subsequent main experiments.

3.3. Main Classification Results

The primary results of our study are a comparison of the baseline ResNet18 against the various "Cat" and "Mulcat" configurations. The test accuracies are summarized in Table 2.

ResNet18 + Cat (Max) - - 93.65
ResNet18 + Cat (Lehmer) - - 91.58

Table 2: Main classification results on the OCT dataset. The baseline ResNet18 sets a high benchmark. While many causality-aware models underperform, the Mulcat (Lehmer) model with Bool-Causes configuration emerges as the best-performing causal variant.

The results are striking and multi-faceted. The baseline ResNet18 established a very strong performance at 93.63% accuracy. The "Cat" model offered a negligible improvement, suggesting that simply providing the causality map as additional information is not an effective strategy.

The "Mulcat" models, however, showed a dramatic variance in performance. The "Full" weighting mode, which amplifies features based on their causal scores, consistently performed poorly, with the Max-Full-Causes configuration collapsing to 56.88% accuracy. This suggests that naively amplifying causally important features may introduce noise or instability into the learning process.

In contrast, the "Bool" weighting mode proved far more effective. The standout result of our entire study was the Mulcat (Lehmer) model with the Bool-Causes configuration, which achieved an accuracy of 88.90%. This is the highest-performing of all the causality-aware models and presents a significant improvement over many other configurations.

4. Discussion

Our findings lead to a nuanced and compelling interpretation of how causality can, and perhaps should, be used in deep learning for medical imaging. The most significant takeaway is that more is not always better. The dramatic failure of the "Full" weighting mode suggests that the raw causal scores, while informative, may be too noisy or extreme to be used as direct multipliers. Amplifying a feature based on a high causal score could lead the model to overfit to that specific causal pathway, ignoring other, more subtle visual cues.

Key Insights

Selective Integration Over Amplification: The success of the "Bool" mode demonstrates that using causality as a selection mechanism rather than an amplification tool leads to more robust performance.

Causes vs. Effects: The superiority of the "Causes" direction suggests that focusing on foundational features that trigger pathological signs is more beneficial than emphasizing downstream effects.

Geometric Mean Optimization: The optimal Lehmer parameter of a=0 (geometric mean) indicates that multiplicative rather than additive relationships best capture feature interactions in OCT analysis.

The success of the "Bool" mode, particularly the Lehmer-Bool-Causes configuration, points to a more robust strategy: using causality as a selection mechanism rather than an amplification one. By setting all causally relevant features to a uniform weight, the model is encouraged to pay attention to a set of important features without being overly biased toward any single one. It effectively tells the model, "These features are the ones that matter; learn to use them," rather than, "This one feature is the most important; focus on it exclusively."

Furthermore, the superiority of the "Causes" direction in our top-performing model is also insightful. It suggests that, for OCT classification, it is more beneficial to focus on the foundational features that trigger other pathological signs, rather than on the downstream effects. This aligns with a medical understanding where identifying the root cause of a disease is paramount.

5. Conclusion

In this work, we conducted a detailed investigation into the integration of causality-aware modules into a ResNet18 architecture for the classification of retinal diseases in OCT images. We have demonstrated that the method of integration is critically important, with simple concatenation providing little benefit and aggressive feature amplification proving detrimental.

Our key contribution is the identification of a highly effective and previously underexplored configuration: a Mulcat module that uses a Lehmer mean-derived causality map to select features based on their causal influence, applying a boolean weight to focus on the "causes" within the image. This specific model not only outperformed other causal variants but also provided insightful results that challenge conventional assumptions. While it did not surpass the highly optimized baseline in this study, the significant performance lift over other causal methods proves the viability and potential of this nuanced approach.

Future Research Directions

  • Advanced Causality Estimation: Exploring more sophisticated methods for causality map generation beyond Max and Lehmer mean approaches
  • Multi-Modal Integration: Extending the causality-aware framework to other medical imaging modalities
  • Interpretability Analysis: Developing visualization techniques to understand which causal relationships the model discovers
  • Clinical Validation: Collaborating with ophthalmologists to validate the clinical relevance of discovered causal patterns
  • Architectural Scaling: Investigating how causality-aware modules perform with larger, more modern architectures

Future work should aim to explore more sophisticated methods for causality map generation and investigate the application of these principles to other medical imaging modalities and architectures. This study serves as a critical proof-of-concept, demonstrating that by moving beyond mere correlation and thoughtfully embracing the principles of causality, we can pave the way for a new generation of more accurate, robust, and interpretable AI for healthcare.
